Physics-Informed Machine Learning for Cross-Surface Droplet Impact Prediction

A leave-one-surface-out study on laser-textured aluminium
Parth SharmaThapar Institute of Engineering and Technology, Patiala, Punjab, India

1The question

Can a model trained on 12 laser-textured surfaces predict how far a droplet spreads on a surface it has never seen?

Droplet-impact models are usually scored on random splits, where every test surface was also in training [2]. Surface design needs the opposite: a prediction before the surface is made.

1–160mPa·s, water to 91 wt% glycerol

Public dataset of Može et al. [1], University of Ljubljana. Target: maximum spreading βmax = Dmax/D0.

Key takeaways

Judge droplet models on unseen surfaces. Random splits hide the gap.
Describe surfaces with quantities every surface has: φ makes the smooth plate the limit of the textured family.
A Gaussian process beats 12 alternatives on unseen surfaces (0.040). Physics backbones and bigger models add no significant gain.
Intervals hold on average, not per fluid, and fail on a different wettability. Say so on the tool.

Methods

2Reading the surface from SEM

Plateaus between laser tracks look like the smooth plate. Track widths w from SEM (about 45 µm deep, 30 µm shallow) give every surface two descriptors:

φ = (1 − w/s)2     Vtex = (1 − φ) h

Textured φ runs from 0.01 (D50) to 0.93 (S800); REF-H is simply φ = 1. Invented spacing/depth codes moved REF-H error by up to 0.022; with φ, ±10 µm in track width moves it by at most 0.005.

3From SEM image to prediction

A 9,070-parameter CNN reads three SEM views (43×, 100×, 350×) of a surface it never saw and estimates φ and Vtex. The GP on these gives 0.038 vs 0.040, better on 8 of 12 surfaces but not significant. REF-H is read as φ = 0.71: no training surface is smoother than 0.93.

4Physics inputs

Re = ρVD0μ
We = ρV2D0σ
βmaxRe−1/5 = √PA + √P

Recomputed Re and We match the dataset to 5×10−7; Oh adds nothing new. The Laan scaling law [3] (P = We Re−2/5) was derived on wettable surfaces: here it reaches only R² = 0.71 and misses unseen surfaces by 11% on average.

5Leakage-safe evaluation

Each condition was repeated five times, so a random split puts near-identical twins in train and test.

1
Grouped 5-fold CV over 297 replicate groups: accuracy on seen surfaces.
2
Leave-one-surface-out, 12 folds: the model-selection criterion.
3
REF-H held out: never used to train or choose a model.

Inputs ln Re, ln We, ln D0, φ, Vtex; target ln βmax. A change is accepted only if its 95% interval from resampling whole surfaces (4,000 draws) excludes zero.

Results

613 models, one test

0.040Gaussian process (Matérn 3/2) on the held-out surface, 1.1% mean error, against 0.056 for XGBoost (95% CI of difference 0.006–0.028). On seen surfaces: 0.036.

7What did not help

Paired surface-bootstrap 95% CIs. A Laan backbone, other kernels, nested selection and CNN descriptors each move the error by under 0.002. Only XGBoost differs significantly, and it is worse. Removing φ and Vtex: 0.040 → 0.046. The simple GP stays.

Future work

  • Measured advancing and receding angles for every fluid–surface pair as an input.
  • Noise that depends on viscosity (Ohnesorge number).
  • New surfaces, with a test set held back before any tuning.

8Knowing when not to trust it

90.1%of unseen-surface impacts fall inside the 90% interval (84–96% per surface), but 76% for water against 98% for 91 wt% glycerol. The OOD check flags D50 and S50.

9Surface by surface

D50 (φ = 0.01, the edge of the range) is hardest for every learner. The descriptors cut its error from 0.091 to 0.059, but hurt on S50 and D200. With 12 surfaces, a hint, not proof.

10Smooth-plate stress test

1.9%mean error (RMSE 0.059), but only 62% inside the interval. Highest-We third over-predicted by 0.041.
Misses sit in water, 20% and 60% glycerol (24–56% coverage); viscous fluids stay at 96%. REF-H is hydrophobic, the textures superhydrophobic: wettability is the missing input. Less friction over trapped air may explain why textures spread further HYPOTHESIS
